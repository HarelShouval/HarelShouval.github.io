A few years ago, we had friends over for dinner, and had a bottle of soda water on the table. Our friends, like us, are of Israeli origin, and many Israelis use SodaStream instead of buying bottles in the store. Our friend, Chuck, asked me why I donÕt use SodaStream, I told him that since SodaStream, at some point, built its factories in the occupied territory of the West Bank, an occupation I oppose, I decided to no longer use SodaStream. Chuck said that he agrees with my views on this, but that on the other hand itÕs better for the environment to use SodaStream because you do not consume plastic bottles, so for him thatÕs more important. This leaves me with a dilemma, to use SodaStream or bottled seltzer, each of these decisions leaves me frustrated. Of course, I could choose to drink tap water, but did I say already that I like soda water, which is another (maybe less righteous) constraint.      The SodaStream dilemma is an example of the types of decisions that we face, where each option leaves us unsatisfied, or frustrated. In many decisions that we make we try to satisfy or even optimize our interests or values or both. In many cases though these different factors are inconsistent with each other, and decisions that maximize one might reduce the other. Given such different and possibly inconsistent constraints, each decision will violate some of the constraints. Violating such constraints is likely to leave us frustrated. I contend that in this world, many of the decisions that we make fall into the category in which external constraints do not allow us to obtain all our goals or desires. Such a world falls into a class of systems that physicists call frustrated systems2. Frustrated systems in physics, have distinct general properties, and I will argue here that this analogy with physics can contribute to how we understand the social world.            Questions regarding frustrating decisions only have a broader impact if my decisions have an impact on the final outcomes. For example, if my decision to use SodaStream or not has an impact on middle eastern politics, or on the environmental fate of the planet. In truth my decisions in the SodaStream example, and in many other cases, will have at best only an infinitesimal impact on many of these problems. However, letÕs assume for now that my decisions can affect the decisions of others, and this might lead to some cascade that could possibly result in noticeable changes to the world. This might be a stretch for this example1, because the system is so large, and I am so small. However, even in large systems, if what I do somehow becomes correlated with what many others do, my choices might matter.            The central goal of this text, apart from trying to justify my decision about which soda to drink, is to claim that such frustrations are actually a central component of how the social world works, and to try and understand what this implies. In order to understand this, I intend to use simple toy-models of the world borrowed from Physics. In other words, my approach here is to gain some insight about our very complicated social world, by using what I consider the ÔsimplerÕ world of model systems explored by physicists.             I know that when I say the words ÔsimpleÕ and ÔphysicsÕ in the same breath some people might immediately switch off. I promise here to do my best to explain these systems intuitively, without using complex mathematics. In following sections I try to explain some of the rudimentary physics of frustrated systems, without using much math. There are some optional math boxes in the appendix, but they will not be essential for understanding the setup of the models presented, and their results. They will be essential if one wants to understand in detail how such models are constructed, and possibly the justification for some of the general theoretical claims made.            I make the strong claim here, that based on the two simple assumptions, outlined above, one can already draw significant conclusions about the nature of the world we live in. The first assumption is simply that many of the decisions we make in this world are inherently and unavoidably frustrating. The second assumption is that our decisions have some effect on the world, and therefore our decisions affect other peopleÕs decisions. These two assumptions imply that the social and political world we live in is inherently a frustrated system.       Once we recognize the world as a frustrated system, we can draw several very general qualitative conclusions. First, in such a frustrated system there are many possible outcomes that can reduce peopleÕs frustrations, but not eliminate them. In frustrated systems there are ways to reduce average frustrations, but compromises are essential, as there is no way to totally eliminate all frustrations. If in such a world there are significant power differentials, frustrations will in general be much larger than in a more egalitarian world. Finally, the purest approach which tries to eliminate all frustrations, which tries to achieve utopia, is not only destined to fail, but also to make matters worse.1.1 Are frustrations inevitable?        While maybe not all decisions have the same life-changing consequences as the decision about which soda to buy, many of the decisions we make do share a central characteristic of that decision. When we choose the state of the chosen spin, we simply yield to the net force. We keep updating the system by repeatedly and randomly choosing another spin and updating its state. This is a paraphrase of a common statement, one often made by so called libertarians. There is a moral dimension to this claim. The moralistic basis of this idea is that people should be rewarded on the basis of merit. Not everyone is equal, some people are smarter, some are physically stronger, some work harder, and morally they should be rewarded for this.             This merit-based approach is a fundamental moral challenge to the egalitarian point of view, people should get different benefits, because people are not equal. This view also claims that by rewarding merit, society as a whole is advanced, because if everyone gets the same benefits, irrespective of their talents or effort, those who are most talented and hard working will have no incentive to work hard, from which society as a whole will suffer.       In order to provide additional benefits on the basis of merit, one needs to know how to measure merit. How do you measure merit, how do you measure talents or effort? One could measure hours worked to measure effort or have standardized tests to measure talent. However, some people might work many hours but not do much, and what do standardize test show, maybe nothing related to the real world. An alternative is to measure merit simply on the basis of success in the real world. The merit-based approach would therefore say, we compensate success as a proxy for merit, and that this is both moral and it will also improve the world.            As an example of this, consider a company with several people working in sales. Each person gets a different region to work in, and some manage to sell more while others less. How much they manage to sell might depend partially on their skill, but it will also depend on the regions that they were allotted, and this is not under their control. Each of them receives bonuses which are proportional to their sales. In addition, the company wants to promote better salespeople, to increase the company sales. To accomplish this the company decides to increase the size of the regions of the more successful salespeople, while reducing the sizes of the regions of those with less merit.             When these people got their positions no one could tell who would be better, there were some indications based on their prior work experience, on their grades in school, but in truth none of these proved to be a good predictor of who would succeed. There was no good measure of merit for this job, so instead what was used as a proxy for merit was actual success.            So how can we modify the egalitarian classroom of the previous chapter to also take merit into account. Remember that in the simulation that we ran Jennifer became less frustrated than all other students, and letÕs assume that Omar was also less frustrated than almost any other student, or in other words they are more successful. Based on this success we will assume Jennifer and Omar have more merit. To implement rewarding merit in our simulations, we will slightly increase the power of the less frustrated students by increasing their influence on the other students. This will be explained below in more detail.            Such a merit-based approach is very common in the real world. For example, people with more money in their bank account do not have to pay fees, they get higher interest rates on savings and lower interest rates on borrowing. Presumably, the balance of your bank account reflects merit, you earn more, spend less or both, and therefore you are more trustworthy from the bank's point of view. In truth though, the bank has a financial incentive to keep you as a customer, independent of why you have a higher balance in your bank account. Trust fund babies according to this metric have just as much merit as people who worked hard and obtained a good bank balance on the basis of their own accomplishment.             In the realm of education, we find that students who previously went to better schools have more chances of being accepted to highly ranked universities, students who went to highly ranked universities get better jobs and higher salaries. The merit-based approach is the norm, and might actually have, shall we say, some merit. However, as shown below, on the basis of these very simple models, this approach exhibits significant flaws.       Figure 4.4: The impact of a Ômerit-basedÕ approach on frustration. A major objection would be that I set up a model in which no true merit exists, and I use it to examine the effect of the consequences of a meritocracy. To a certain extent this criticism of the model is reasonable, though it misses the major point made here. The major point of this exercise is that there is danger in conflating success with merit, and that it is often very hard to tell them apart. In the model I set up here, there is no real merit, all merit measured arose from luck, from who happened to be less frustrated initially. In the real world in contrast, merit does exist.             Although merit does exist in the real world, in some cases, true merit seems to be easier to measure than in others. For example, in the field of athletics success is really very closely related to merit (but see below the discussion about relative age effect). In other fields like the stock market, chance plays a large role in success. If we measure merit by success, we usually do not know what part of success arose from merit, and what part arose from luck.             In the real world apart from luck there are many other factors that influence success, many of them related to some form of inheritance. In our world the starting positions of people depends on their parentsÕ wealth, education, and connections, and these starting positions have a large impact on success. Educational opportunities, for example are closely related to wealth, and education clearly affects our ability to succeed in many, though not all fields. Another example is that the type of jobs one gets in practice are clearly connected to our parentsÕ connections, and the social circles we live in. The juicy term Ònepotism babiesÓ or Ònepo babiesÓ seems to capture many of these issues. Nepo babies are often quite talented, they have merit, but there are many other people who are just as talented but did not get the same chances.       I would like to emphasize again the main point I am making here, the main cautionary comment made in this section. I am not claiming that true merit does not exist in the world, I am warning against rewarding merit on the basis of success, however often we have no other way of measuring merit. Success in most real-world situations comes from various sources, only some of them related to Ôreal-meritÕ. What the merit-based classroom model shows is that a social system that rewards success as a proxy for merit quickly becomes indistinguishable from a rigid oligarchy.            These results suggest a seemingly simple solution, lets measure merit, independently from success, and reward true merit rather than fake merit, thus avoiding the deleterious consequences resulting from rewarding success. We are left we the seemingly simpler problem of how evaluate true merit.       (Relate to the Mathew effect, here or in chapter discussion section)      4.4	 Measuring Success      How can that be done? One of the points I am making, is that the class of models with a single optimal solution are inherently too simple to be insightful descriptions of the world. Although this model is very simple, itÕs behavior is actually not that simple. The behavior of this model allows for many possible final outcomes, not all outcomes are possible, indeed most are not. Yet, there are many possible outcomes, which are statistically similar, but differ in the details.    One of the main points of this whole exercise is to put forward models that are a little more complex than the simplest deterministic models sometimes assumed. Such simple models usually have a single possible outcome, an optimal point where history ends and this final state of the world can be predicted. Such theories in which there is a single optimal outcome, and in which, in the end the best system wins, have a prominent place in political thinking. This domain of single outcome theories, or a better term might be ideologies, includes Capitalism, Communism, and many of the worldÕs other religions.        The major theme of this practice is therefore: letÕs try to consider models of world that have frustrations. Such systems, even if they are defined by a set of very simple rules, like Òthe democratic classroomÓ, generate a rich landscape, where there are many possible final outcomes.           In addition to having many possible similar final outcomes, none of these final outcomes are great, as everyone remains quite frustrated, at least some of the time. Despite the simplicity of the model explored, these central features that I outlined are actually general properties of frustrated systems. The general properties outlined here are the point, and although the model details are obviously simplistic, not realistic or even plain wrong in some sense, these universal properties remain a feature of frustrated systems even with less simplistic assumptions. Though I believe that the social world is a frustrated system, I cannot prove it. The main point here it to get the readers to entertain this metaphor and explore with me the implications of this way of thought.           Personally, I am intrigued by such models, not because of what I like about them, but precisely because of what I do not like. I would like to live in a world where there is a single optimal solution in which everybody is happy. I like systems with a single optimal solution, because I tend to like simplicity, and yes, IÕd rather be in a system where happiness is guaranteed.              This promise, that happiness can be guaranteed, is what many of the worldÕs religions and ideologies promise us. Or maybe they do not promise us guaranteed happiness, but if we follow their prescriptions, we will achieve happiness. If we follow what the ideology tells us to do, we will achieve happiness in this world. If we follow what the Priest, Rabbi, Imam, or Guru tell us we are guaranteed to obtain eternal happiness in some fantasy afterlife. I would include ÒScientific MarxismÓ as one of these religions.            Similarly, adherents of fundamentalist capitalism tell us that if you work hard and are greedy enough you will obtain wealth, which is synonymous with happiness according to the postulates of that religion, and this will not only make us happy, but the world in general.             Unfortunately, in contrast to these utopian world views, a frustrated model of the world does not have a singular optimal state and in each of the many optimal states, many people remain unhappy, even if by construction this world is egalitarian.      	You might think that I forgot the main question motivating this whole exercise, as you might remember, is about whether to use SodaStream.
